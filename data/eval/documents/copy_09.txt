Neoclassical economic theory posits that individual decision-makers act as rational utility maximizers equipped with unlimited computational capacity and stable preferences. Behavioral economics challenges this axiomatic framework by documenting systematic cognitive biases that diverge predictably from normative rationality. Prospect theory demonstrates that agents evaluate potential outcomes as gains or losses relative to an arbitrary reference point rather than final wealth states. Furthermore, the empirical value function displays S-shaped curvature, exhibiting diminishing sensitivity and pronounced loss aversion, where subjective disutility from losses substantially exceeds equivalent monetary gains. Additional bounded rationality phenomena, such as hyperbolic temporal discounting and framing effects, significantly alter equilibrium pricing and intertemporal savings behaviors.
