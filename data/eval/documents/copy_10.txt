Bacterial biofilms are structured microbial consortia encased within a self-produced matrix of extracellular polymeric substances, including polysaccharides, structural proteins, and extracellular DNA. Biofilm architecture creates microenvironmental gradients of nutrient concentration, pH, and oxygen saturation, establishing distinct physiological niches and slow-growing persister cell populations. Within clinical settings, biofilms adhere tenaciously to indwelling medical prosthetics and mucosal tissues, displaying remarkable tolerance toward conventional antimicrobial therapies up to a thousandfold higher than planktonic counterparts. Physical diffusion barriers retard antibiotic penetration, while enzymatic deactivation within the matrix and elevated rates of horizontal gene transfer foster the rapid dissemination of multidrug-resistant genetic determinants across resident species.
