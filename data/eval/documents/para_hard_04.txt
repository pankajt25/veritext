Biological conversion of atmospheric carbon into stable organic sugars occurs via enzymatic cycles inside autotrophic chloroplasts. The primary entry point involves the enzyme RuBisCO attaching carbon dioxide to five-carbon ribulose molecules, yielding short-lived intermediates that immediately split into pairs of three-carbon acids. Cellular energetic equivalents like ATP and NADPH, accumulated during earlier thylakoid light absorption, subsequently reduce these acids into triose phosphates for eventual carbohydrate synthesis and substrate regeneration. However, thermal stress often aggravates enzymatic inefficiency because oxygen readily competes with carbon dioxide at the catalytic center. This wasteful photorespiratory flux burns metabolic reserves, prompting divergent plant lineages in arid or tropical biomes to evolve compartmentalized spatial and temporal adaptations, including specialized C4 Kranz anatomy and nocturnal CAM acid storage.
