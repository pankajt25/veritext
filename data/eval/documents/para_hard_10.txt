Pathogenic bacteria often aggregate into cooperative surface communities encapsulated within a secreted matrix composed of extracellular polysaccharides, proteins, and ambient nucleic acids. The dense physical structure of these biofilms establishes localized chemical microclimates with steep gradients in pH, oxygen concentration, and nutrient availability, fostering metabolically dormant persister subpopulations. In hospital settings, these adherent mats colonize medical implants and mucosal linings, conferring profound resistance against standard antibiotic treatments at concentrations hundreds of times higher than those lethal to free-floating planktonic bacteria. The polymer scaffold physically hinders drug diffusion, while resident protective enzymes degrade therapeutic agents and elevated rates of lateral gene exchange accelerate the rapid dissemination of antibiotic resistance across the mixed microbial population.
