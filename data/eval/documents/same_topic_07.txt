The foundations of quantum mechanics have long wrestled with the measurement problem, which asks why deterministic, linear wave function evolution appears to yield definite, random classical outcomes upon observation. Quantum decoherence theory explains this apparent collapse by recognizing that realistic quantum systems are never completely isolated from their ambient environment. Constant interactions with surrounding air molecules, thermal radiation, and stray photons induce macroscopic quantum entanglement, rapidly leaking relative phase information into environmental degrees of freedom. As a result, off-diagonal elements in the system reduced density matrix vanish almost instantaneously, picking out stable pointer states that mimic classical probability mixtures without invoking non-unitary state reduction.
