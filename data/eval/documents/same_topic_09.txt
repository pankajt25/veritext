Standard microeconomic models traditionally assume that individuals make choices as rational economic agents who evaluate prospects solely to maximize expected utility. Behavioral economists, led by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, demonstrated that human judgment systematically deviates from normative rationality through cognitive heuristics and context dependencies. Prospect theory reveals that people assess decision outcomes relative to a subjective reference point rather than absolute final asset levels. Furthermore, the empirical value function is concave for perceived gains but convex and distinctly steeper for perceived losses, reflecting profound loss aversion where psychological pain from losing exceeds satisfaction from an equivalent gain. Heuristics like mental accounting, availability, and framing further explain deviations in financial markets and consumer spending.
