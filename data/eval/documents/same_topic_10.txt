Bacterial communities in clinical and natural settings predominantly exist not as free-swimming planktonic cells, but as sessile multicellular biofilms attached to abiotic surfaces or biological tissues. Within a biofilm, microorganisms secrete an extracellular matrix composed of exopolysaccharides, structural proteins, and extracellular nucleic acids that protect the resident microcolonies. Microenvironmental gradients within the biofilm matrix limit oxygen diffusion and nutrient availability, causing sub-populations of bacteria to enter a dormant, slow-metabolizing persister state. Consequently, biofilms exhibit extreme tolerance to antibiotic treatments and host immune responses, frequently causing chronic, recalcitrant infections on catheter implants, artificial joints, and dental surfaces while promoting rapid horizontal transfer of resistance plasmids among embedded bacterial strains.
