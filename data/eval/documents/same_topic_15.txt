Deep-sea hydrothermal vents discovered along tectonic mid-ocean ridges sustain extraordinary biological communities completely disconnected from sunlight and terrestrial photosynthesis. Seawater filtering downward through basaltic fissures near sub-seafloor magma chambers becomes superheated above three hundred degrees Celsius, dissolving mineral sulfides and volatile gases from mantle rocks. When this acidic, mineral-laden fluid erupts back into the near-freezing abyssal water, rapid thermal quenching precipitates iron and copper sulfides into tall hydrothermal chimneys known as black smokers. Chemolithoautotrophic bacteria and archaea thrive in this extreme environment, deriving biochemical energy by oxidizing reduced hydrogen sulfide or methane to fix inorganic carbon, establishing a trophic pyramid that supports giant red-plumed tube worms and specialized vent crustaceans.
