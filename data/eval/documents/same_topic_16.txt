Direct air capture represents an engineered carbon removal pathway designed to separate carbon dioxide molecules directly from ambient atmospheric air rather than industrial point sources. Because atmospheric carbon dioxide is present at a low concentration of roughly 420 parts per million, contactors must circulate enormous volumes of air across specialized reactive sorbents. Liquid solvent systems capture carbon dioxide using strongly alkaline potassium hydroxide solutions to form carbonate salts, which are subsequently precipitated as calcium carbonate pellets and calcined in high-temperature kilns to yield pure carbon dioxide gas. In contrast, solid sorbent systems utilize porous filter monoliths functionalized with basic amine chemical groups, releasing captured carbon dioxide during low-temperature vacuum thermal desorption. The concentrated gas is then permanently injected into subterranean basalt formations.
