The codification of mathematical linear perspective during the fifteenth-century Italian Renaissance revolutionized pictorial space by establishing a geometric method for projecting three-dimensional reality onto a flat two-dimensional plane. Pioneered empirically by Florentine architect Filippo Brunelleschi and codified in Leon Battista Alberti treatise On Painting, the technique constructs a coherent illusion of depth through systematically converging lines. Orthogonals extend from the viewer eye through a picture plane to meet at a single central vanishing point located along an eye-level horizon line. Transverse lines spaced at geometrically decreasing intervals simulate the foreshortening of objects receding into background space. This visual framework reflected humanism belief that rational geometry could organize human perception and accurately represent the natural world.
