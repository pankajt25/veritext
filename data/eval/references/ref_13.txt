Synaptic plasticity constitutes the neurobiological substrate underlying learning and memory formation in mammalian neural circuits. Long-term potentiation, initially characterized in hippocampal pyramidal neurons, involves a persistent strengthening of synaptic transmission efficacy following high-frequency afferent stimulation. The primary induction trigger requires postsynaptic membrane depolarization sufficient to dislodge magnesium ions blocking the pore of N-methyl-D-aspartate receptors. Influx of calcium ions through these unblocked receptors activates calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II, which phosphorylates existing alpha-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid receptors and facilitates the trafficking of additional receptor subunits into the postsynaptic density, elevating excitatory postsynaptic currents.
